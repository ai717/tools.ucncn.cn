📖 Understanding Chinese Financial Numerals (Dàxiě): Origin, Anti-Fraud & Accounting Rules

In Chinese commerce, banking, and official invoicing, monetary amounts must be written in formal complex Chinese numerals, known as Dàxiě (Capital Numerals). Originating during the Ming Dynasty under Emperor Zhu Yuanzhang after a major government embezzlement scandal, this system was specifically invented to prevent forgery and unauthorized document tampering.

❓ Why are simple Chinese numbers prohibited on official checks?

Standard everyday Chinese numbers (Yī, Èr, Sān, Shí) consist of very few strokes. A dishonest party could easily add a single vertical stroke to turn 1 into 10, or add strokes to alter simple digits into higher magnitudes. Complex financial numerals like 壹 (1), 贰 (2), 叁 (3), and 拾 (10) cannot be altered by adding simple brush or pen strokes.

❓ What are the rules for Zero (零) and currency suffixes?

According to the People's Bank of China regulations:
• If an amount has no fractional cents (Jiao and Fen), the suffix 整 (Zhěng) or Zhèng must be appended at the end (e.g. 壹佰元整).
• If an amount has Jiao but no Fen, 整 may be omitted or included.
• Consecutive interior zeros are collapsed into a single 零 (e.g. 1005 becomes 壹仟零伍元).

❓ What are the sub-currency units Yuan, Jiao, and Fen?

Chinese Yuan (Yuan / RMB) is divided into 10 Jiao (角), and each Jiao is divided into 10 Fen (分). Therefore:
• 1 Yuan = 10 Jiao = 100 Fen.
• ¥125.85 is read: 壹佰贰拾伍元捌角伍分.
